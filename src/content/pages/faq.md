## Tax & Thresholds

<details class="mb-3 pb-3" style="border-bottom:1px solid var(--govborder);">
<summary class="h6 mb-0">My pay went up but it feels like I lost more of it to tax — what's going on?</summary>
<p class="mt-2 mb-0" style="max-width:70ch;">That's fiscal drag at work. The £12,570 Personal Allowance and the £50,270 Higher Rate threshold have stayed put since 2021/22, and the Budget in November 2025 pushed that freeze out to April 2031. Salaries don't stand still the same way — so a pay rise that only keeps pace with inflation still moves more of your income into taxed territory, or across the boundary into the 40% band, purely because the goalposts haven't moved. No rate has gone up; the tax take rises anyway.</p>
</details>

<details class="mb-3 pb-3" style="border-bottom:1px solid var(--govborder);">
<summary class="h6 mb-0">I just crossed £100,000 — why did my take-home pay barely move (or fall)?</summary>
<p class="mt-2 mb-0" style="max-width:70ch;">Between £100,000 and £125,140 of "adjusted net income", you don't just pay 40% Income Tax — you also lose £1 of your £12,570 Personal Allowance for every £2 you earn, until it's gone entirely at £125,140. Stack the two together and the effective rate on that slice of pay lands near 60%. A £1,000 rise inside that band can shrink to roughly £400 after deductions. Pension contributions or Gift Aid that bring your adjusted net income back under £100,000 are the usual way people sidestep this band.</p>
</details>

<details class="mb-3 pb-3" style="border-bottom:1px solid var(--govborder);">
<summary class="h6 mb-0">Scottish tax keeps diverging from the rest of the UK — how far apart are they now?</summary>
<p class="mt-2 mb-0" style="max-width:70ch;">Since 2017 Holyrood has set its own Income Tax rates, and the gap has widened every year. Scotland now runs six bands — Starter 19%, Basic 20%, Intermediate 21%, Higher 42%, Advanced 45%, Top 48% — against three bands (20/40/45) for England, Wales and Northern Ireland. This year the Scottish Basic and Intermediate thresholds jumped 7.4%, comfortably ahead of inflation, while Higher, Advanced and Top stayed frozen. The Advanced band was also stretched so 45% now runs all the way to £125,140, lining up with where the rest-of-UK Additional rate begins. National Insurance doesn't change with postcode — that stays identical across the whole UK.</p>
</details>

<details class="mb-3 pb-3" style="border-bottom:1px solid var(--govborder);">
<summary class="h6 mb-0">Employer NI went up — does that make Salary Sacrifice less attractive for me?</summary>
<p class="mt-2 mb-0" style="max-width:70ch;">The opposite, if anything. Employers now pay 15% National Insurance (up from 13.8%) on pay above just £5,000 a year, down from a £9,100 threshold — both changes have applied since April 2025 and carry through unchanged. Salary you sacrifice, typically into a pension, still escapes your Income Tax and your 8%/2% employee NI, and now escapes a bigger 15% employer NI charge too. Because employers save more per pound sacrificed than before, many choose to funnel some of that extra saving into your pension rather than keep it, which is why sacrifice schemes have generally become more generous, not less.</p>
</details>

<details class="mb-3 pb-3" style="border-bottom:1px solid var(--govborder);">
<summary class="h6 mb-0">Is it true bonuses get hit with a special, higher tax rate?</summary>
<p class="mt-2 mb-0" style="max-width:70ch;">No — that's a payslip illusion, not a real rule. A bonus is just added to that period's pay and run through the same PAYE system as your normal salary. Because PAYE assumes each month looks like a twelfth of your annual pay, a big bonus in one month can temporarily look like a much higher annual salary, pushing that month into a steeper band and making the deduction seem huge. HMRC's cumulative PAYE calculation sorts this out automatically over the rest of the tax year — the extra tax withheld comes back to you through smaller deductions later, or as a refund.</p>
</details>

<details class="mb-3 pb-3" style="border-bottom:1px solid var(--govborder);">
<summary class="h6 mb-0">A colleague earns exactly what I do but takes home more — why?</summary>
<p class="mt-2 mb-0" style="max-width:70ch;">Same gross salary doesn't guarantee the same net pay. Differences usually trace back to: the tax code each of you has been issued (shaped by benefits in kind, a second job, or HMRC recovering an old underpayment), whether one of you is a Scottish taxpayer, whether either of you carries a student loan and on which plan, and how your pension contributions are structured — salary sacrifice lowers your taxable pay in a way that a standard "relief at source" pension doesn't. Any of these on their own can produce a noticeably different take-home figure from an identical headline salary.</p>
</details>

## Pensions & Salary Sacrifice

<details class="mb-3 pb-3" style="border-bottom:1px solid var(--govborder);">
<summary class="h6 mb-0">My payslip shows a lower pension contribution than I expected — why?</summary>
<p class="mt-2 mb-0" style="max-width:70ch;">Auto-enrolment contributions usually aren't calculated on your whole salary. Most schemes use "qualifying earnings" — the slice between £6,240 and £50,270 — so the first £6,240 is ignored completely. On a £32,000 salary with a 5% employee contribution, you're not contributing 5% of £32,000; you're contributing 5% of £25,760 (£32,000 minus £6,240), which comes to £1,288 rather than the £1,600 you might have expected. Some employers choose to calculate contributions on total pay instead — worth checking your scheme booklet, since it's more generous than the legal minimum.</p>
</details>

<details class="mb-3 pb-3" style="border-bottom:1px solid var(--govborder);">
<summary class="h6 mb-0">What actually happens to my pay when I agree to salary sacrifice?</summary>
<p class="mt-2 mb-0" style="max-width:70ch;">You formally agree to a lower contractual salary, and your employer pays the difference straight into your pension instead. Because that slice of pay never appears as your earnings, it isn't charged Income Tax or your 8%/2% employee National Insurance — and your employer avoids their 15% NI on it too. The upshot is that giving up, say, £3,000 a year of salary typically costs you noticeably less than £3,000 in take-home pay, because you're saving tax and NI on every pound sacrificed. The trade-off worth knowing: some lenders and insurers use your reduced contractual salary as your "reference income", which can matter for a mortgage application or income-based benefits like Statutory Maternity Pay.</p>
</details>

<details class="mb-3 pb-3" style="border-bottom:1px solid var(--govborder);">
<summary class="h6 mb-0">Is a "relief at source" pension worse than salary sacrifice?</summary>
<p class="mt-2 mb-0" style="max-width:70ch;">It works differently rather than being simply worse. With relief at source, you pay into your pension from taxed income, and the pension provider claims back 20% from HMRC automatically — a Higher or Additional rate taxpayer then has to claim the rest of their relief through Self Assessment, which is a step people commonly forget. Crucially, relief at source doesn't touch your National Insurance bill at all, since NI is calculated before the pension contribution happens. Salary sacrifice sidesteps both issues: relief is automatic and immediate, and NI is saved as well as tax. For most employees offered the choice, salary sacrifice comes out ahead — the main reason to prefer relief at source is if a lower contractual salary would cause a problem elsewhere.</p>
</details>

## National Insurance

<details class="mb-3 pb-3" style="border-bottom:1px solid var(--govborder);">
<summary class="h6 mb-0">National Insurance keeps taking a bigger bite of my payslip — has the rate gone up?</summary>
<p class="mt-2 mb-0" style="max-width:70ch;">The headline employee rate has actually fallen — it was 12% as recently as 2021/22 and is 8% today. What's changed is the threshold it starts from: the Primary Threshold has been frozen at £12,570 since 2021/22 and, following the November 2025 Budget, will stay there until April 2031. As wages rise against a fixed starting point, a growing share of your pay sits above that line and gets charged NI, even though the rate itself hasn't moved — the same fiscal drag effect that applies to Income Tax.</p>
</details>

<details class="mb-3 pb-3" style="border-bottom:1px solid var(--govborder);">
<summary class="h6 mb-0">I'm self-employed — do I still pay Class 2 National Insurance?</summary>
<p class="mt-2 mb-0" style="max-width:70ch;">Not directly, in most cases. Since April 2024, Class 2 is no longer collected as a separate payment for people with profits above the Small Profits Threshold (£7,105 for 2026/27) — you're simply treated as having paid it, which keeps your State Pension record intact without an extra bill. If your profits fall below that threshold, you can choose to pay a voluntary weekly rate of £3.65 to avoid a gap in your NI record; for many low-profit years this is one of the cheapest ways to protect a qualifying year towards your pension.</p>
</details>

<details class="mb-3 pb-3" style="border-bottom:1px solid var(--govborder);">
<summary class="h6 mb-0">How does National Insurance work if I run a small business through Self Assessment?</summary>
<p class="mt-2 mb-0" style="max-width:70ch;">As a sole trader, you pay Class 4 NI on your profits — not on your turnover, and not through PAYE. For 2026/27 that's 6% on profits between £12,570 and £50,270, then 2% on anything above. So on £45,000 of profit, you'd pay 6% of £32,430 (profit above £12,570), which comes to £1,945.80, all settled alongside your Income Tax through your Self Assessment return by 31 January. There's no separate employer-style charge to worry about, since you don't have an employer paying the 15% rate on your behalf.</p>
</details>

## Student Loans & Benefits

<details class="mb-3 pb-3" style="border-bottom:1px solid var(--govborder);">
<summary class="h6 mb-0">Which Student Loan threshold applies to me this year?</summary>
<p class="mt-2 mb-0" style="max-width:70ch;">It depends on your plan. For 2026/27: Plan 1 starts repayments at £26,900, Plan 2 at £29,385, Plan 4 (Scotland) at £33,795, Plan 5 at £25,000, and the Postgraduate Loan at £21,000, a figure that hasn't moved since it launched. Above your threshold you repay 9% of the excess (6% on a Postgraduate Loan — 15% combined if you're repaying both an undergraduate plan and a Postgraduate Loan at once). Worth flagging: the Plan 2 threshold has just risen to £29,385, but the government has already announced it will be locked at that level from April 2027 through to April 2030.</p>
</details>

<details class="mb-3 pb-3" style="border-bottom:1px solid var(--govborder);">
<summary class="h6 mb-0">Did Child Benefit really switch to a household income test this year?</summary>
<p class="mt-2 mb-0" style="max-width:70ch;">No — that change was floated in 2024 for an April 2026 launch, then quietly dropped at the Autumn 2024 Budget. The High Income Child Benefit Charge still looks only at the higher earner's individual adjusted net income, not the household's combined total. It kicks in at £60,000, claws back 1% of Child Benefit for every £200 earned above that, and wipes it out completely at £80,000. So the old quirk persists: two parents each earning £59,000 keep every penny of Child Benefit, while one parent alone on £80,000+ gets none — nothing about that has changed for 2026/27.</p>
</details>

<details class="mb-3 pb-3" style="border-bottom:1px solid var(--govborder);">
<summary class="h6 mb-0">How can a tiny pay rise leave me worse off overall?</summary>
<p class="mt-2 mb-0" style="max-width:70ch;">It's rarely the Income Tax bands themselves — it's a benefit or allowance that disappears in one lump rather than tapering gradually. Nudging past £60,000 starts the Child Benefit clawback, and a jump from, say, £79,500 to £80,500 can erase what's left of it in a single step. Crossing £100,000 starts eroding your Personal Allowance and, for parents, ends eligibility for free childcare hours and Tax-Free Childcare outright rather than reducing it bit by bit. A small increase in gross pay near any of these lines can cost you far more in lost support than the raise itself is worth.</p>
</details>

## Family & Marriage Allowance

<details class="mb-3 pb-3" style="border-bottom:1px solid var(--govborder);">
<summary class="h6 mb-0">My partner doesn't work — can that lower my tax bill?</summary>
<p class="mt-2 mb-0" style="max-width:70ch;">Possibly, through Marriage Allowance. If your spouse or civil partner earns under £12,570, they can transfer £1,260 of their unused Personal Allowance to you, provided you're a Basic Rate taxpayer (broadly £12,571–£50,270 for the rest of the UK). That transfer is worth up to £252 a year in reduced Income Tax. It's an all-or-nothing transfer of £1,260 — you can't split it — and you can backdate a claim up to four tax years if you were eligible but never applied, potentially producing a lump sum on top of the ongoing saving. It's not available once the receiving partner becomes a Higher Rate taxpayer.</p>
</details>

<details class="mb-3 pb-3" style="border-bottom:1px solid var(--govborder);">
<summary class="h6 mb-0">What is "adjusted net income" and why does it keep coming up?</summary>
<p class="mt-2 mb-0" style="max-width:70ch;">It's the figure HMRC actually uses to test several allowances and charges — not your gross salary. Broadly, it's your total taxable income minus things like gross pension contributions and Gift Aid donations (grossed up). This matters because the Personal Allowance taper above £100,000, the High Income Child Benefit Charge above £60,000, and Marriage Allowance eligibility are all judged against adjusted net income rather than headline pay. That's why increasing pension contributions can pull someone back under one of these thresholds even though their salary on paper hasn't changed at all.</p>
</details>

## Self-Employment

<details class="mb-3 pb-3" style="border-bottom:1px solid var(--govborder);">
<summary class="h6 mb-0">How is tax worked out if I'm self-employed rather than on a payslip?</summary>
<p class="mt-2 mb-0" style="max-width:70ch;">There's no PAYE deducting anything automatically — instead, you report your profit (income minus allowable business costs) through Self Assessment, and pay Income Tax and Class 4 NI together by 31 January following the end of the tax year. On, say, £48,000 profit after expenses, the Income Tax works out at roughly £7,086 (20% on income between £12,570 and £48,000), and Class 4 NI adds a further 6% on profit above £12,570 — about £2,126 — bringing total deductions to around £9,212, leaving roughly £38,788. Because nothing is withheld as you go, most self-employed people also make "payments on account" twice a year toward the following year's bill, which catches many first-timers off guard.</p>
</details>

<details class="mb-3 pb-3" style="border-bottom:1px solid var(--govborder);">
<summary class="h6 mb-0">What counts as an allowable business expense?</summary>
<p class="mt-2 mb-0" style="max-width:70ch;">Broadly, anything spent "wholly and exclusively" for the business — equipment, business travel, professional subscriptions, accountancy fees, and a reasonable share of home costs if you work from home (HMRC allows a simplified flat rate, currently £6 a week, without needing to keep detailed bills). Vehicle costs can be claimed either as actual running costs or via the simplified mileage rate (45p per mile for the first 10,000 business miles, 25p after). Deducting a legitimate expense doesn't just lower your Income Tax — it lowers the profit figure Class 4 NI is calculated on too, so it's worth keeping receipts even for smaller items.</p>
</details>

## Advanced Planning

<details class="mb-3 pb-3" style="border-bottom:1px solid var(--govborder);">
<summary class="h6 mb-0">A £5,000 raise near £100,000 barely moved my take-home pay — what happened?</summary>
<p class="mt-2 mb-0" style="max-width:70ch;">You've likely hit the Personal Allowance taper. Above £100,000 of adjusted net income, you lose £1 of your £12,570 allowance for every £2 you earn, on top of paying 40% Income Tax and 2% NI on that income — an effective marginal rate close to 60% inside the £100,000–£125,140 band. A £5,000 rise entirely within that band can leave you with only around £2,000 extra after deductions. Redirecting some or all of that rise into a pension via salary sacrifice brings your adjusted net income back down, restoring the allowance and usually leaving you meaningfully better off between the pension pot and the tax saved combined.</p>
</details>
