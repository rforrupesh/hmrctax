# What Is Tax? A Simple Guide to How Tax Works in the UK

Tax is money collected by the government from individuals and businesses to help pay for public services and government spending. In the UK, people may pay tax on their income, purchases, property, investments and other types of economic activity.

The amount of tax you pay depends on factors such as your income, where you live, what you earn money from and which allowances or deductions you qualify for.

If you are trying to understand your payslip or estimate your take-home pay, you can use our [UK Tax Calculator](/uk-tax-calculator/) to see an estimate based on your income.

## What Does Tax Mean?

In simple terms, **tax is a compulsory payment to the government**.

For example, if you work for an employer, Income Tax may be deducted from your salary before you receive your pay. Employees may also have National Insurance deducted.

Businesses can pay different taxes, while consumers normally pay taxes such as VAT when buying many goods and services.

The government uses tax revenue to help fund services and spending across the UK, including areas such as healthcare, education, transport, defence and public administration.

## How Does Tax Work in the UK?

The UK tax system contains several different types of taxes. Each tax has its own rules, rates, thresholds and allowances.

For an employee, the process may look like this:

1. You earn a salary from your employer.
2. Your employer calculates your taxable pay.
3. Income Tax is deducted through PAYE when applicable.
4. National Insurance may also be deducted.
5. You receive your remaining amount as take-home pay.

For example, someone earning £40,000 a year does not normally pay Income Tax on the entire £40,000 if they qualify for the standard Personal Allowance.

## What Is Income Tax?

**Income Tax** is a tax on certain types of income.

For many employees, Income Tax is deducted automatically from their salary through the **Pay As You Earn (PAYE)** system.

For the 2025/26 tax year, the standard Personal Allowance is £12,570 for people who qualify for the full allowance.

For England, Wales and Northern Ireland, the main Income Tax rates are:

| Tax band | Taxable income | Rate |
|---|---:|---:|
| Personal Allowance | Up to £12,570 | 0% |
| Basic rate | £12,571–£50,270 | 20% |
| Higher rate | £50,271–£125,140 | 40% |
| Additional rate | Over £125,140 | 45% |

These figures are for the standard tax situation. Scotland has different Income Tax bands and rates for most non-savings and non-dividend income.

You can read more about the current bands in our guide to [UK Tax Rates](/uk-tax-rates/).

## What Is the Personal Allowance?

The **Personal Allowance** is the amount of income most eligible individuals can receive before paying Income Tax.

For 2025/26, the standard Personal Allowance is **£12,570**.

For example, if you earn £30,000 and receive the full £12,570 Personal Allowance:

**£30,000 − £12,570 = £17,430 taxable income**

At the basic Income Tax rate of 20%, the Income Tax calculation would be:

**£17,430 × 20% = £3,486**

This is a simplified Income Tax calculation and does not include National Insurance, pension deductions, student loan repayments or other adjustments.

## What Is National Insurance?

**National Insurance (NI)** is separate from Income Tax.

Employees can pay National Insurance on employment earnings once their earnings reach the relevant threshold. The money contributes toward certain state benefits and services.

For 2025/26, the main employee National Insurance rate is 8% between the relevant Primary Threshold and Upper Earnings Limit, with a 2% rate applying above the Upper Earnings Limit.

Your payslip may therefore show two separate deductions:

| Deduction | What it is |
|---|---|
| Income Tax | Tax on taxable income |
| National Insurance | Contribution based on relevant earnings |

This is why your total deductions can be higher than your Income Tax alone.

## What Are the Main Types of Tax in the UK?

There are many different taxes in the UK. Some of the most commonly encountered include:

### Income Tax

Income Tax applies to taxable income from employment, self-employment, pensions, savings and other sources.

### National Insurance

National Insurance is generally charged on earnings and certain self-employed profits, depending on the person's circumstances.

### VAT

**Value Added Tax (VAT)** is a consumption tax added to many goods and services.

Businesses that are registered for VAT generally charge VAT to customers and account for it to HM Revenue & Customs (HMRC).

The standard VAT rate is **20%**, although reduced and zero rates apply to certain goods and services.

### Capital Gains Tax

Capital Gains Tax may apply when you sell or dispose of an asset that has increased in value.

Examples can include certain shares, investments and property.

The tax is generally based on the gain rather than the total amount received from selling the asset.

### Corporation Tax

Corporation Tax is a tax on the taxable profits of companies and certain other organisations.

It is different from Income Tax, which generally applies to individuals.

### Inheritance Tax

Inheritance Tax can apply to an estate after someone dies when the value of the estate exceeds the relevant tax-free thresholds and exemptions.

The rules can be complicated because exemptions, reliefs and the composition of an estate can affect the amount due.

## Direct Tax vs Indirect Tax

Taxes can also be described as **direct** or **indirect** taxes.

| Type | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Direct tax | Generally paid directly by the taxpayer to the government | Income Tax |
| Indirect tax | Generally collected through the purchase of goods or services | VAT |

Income Tax is a common example of a direct tax because it is charged on an individual's taxable income.

VAT is an indirect tax because it is normally included in the price paid for goods and services.

## Why Does the Government Collect Tax?

Governments collect taxes to raise revenue for public spending.

Tax revenue can help finance areas such as:

- Healthcare
- Education
- Roads and transport
- Defence
- Policing
- Social security
- Public administration
- Other government services

The exact way tax revenue is spent changes over time according to government budgets and spending decisions.

## How Is Your Tax Calculated?

Your tax bill depends on more than just your salary.

Some factors that can affect the calculation include:

- Annual income
- Taxable benefits
- Personal Allowance
- Tax code
- Pension contributions
- Savings income
- Dividend income
- Capital gains
- Student loan repayments
- Employment status
- Scottish taxpayer status
- Other allowances and reliefs

For example, two people earning the same gross salary may have different take-home pay because their pension contributions, tax codes or other deductions are different.

## Example of a Simple Tax Calculation

Suppose an employee earns **£35,000** during a tax year and receives the standard £12,570 Personal Allowance.

Their simplified taxable income would be:

**£35,000 − £12,570 = £22,430**

If all of this taxable income falls within the basic-rate band:

**£22,430 × 20% = £4,486**

So the simplified Income Tax calculation is £4,486.

However, this is **not the same as total payroll deductions**. National Insurance and other deductions may also reduce the employee's take-home pay.

## What Is a Tax Year?

A UK tax year is the period used to calculate an individual's tax position.

For individuals, the UK tax year normally runs from **6 April to 5 April** of the following year.

For example:

| Tax year | Start | End |
|---|---|---|
| 2024/25 | 6 April 2024 | 5 April 2025 |
| 2025/26 | 6 April 2025 | 5 April 2026 |
| 2026/27 | 6 April 2026 | 5 April 2027 |

This is different from the calendar year, which runs from January to December.

Understanding the tax year is important when comparing tax rates, allowances and income.

## Who Collects Tax in the UK?

**HM Revenue & Customs (HMRC)** is the UK government department responsible for administering and collecting many taxes.

HMRC manages areas including Income Tax, National Insurance, VAT, Corporation Tax and various other taxes and duties.

The official GOV.UK website provides information about current tax rates, allowances, thresholds and tax obligations.

[Check Income Tax rates and allowances on GOV.UK](https://www.gov.uk/income-tax-rates)

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is tax compulsory in the UK?

Taxes are generally compulsory when the relevant legal conditions apply. Whether someone has to pay a particular tax depends on their income, transactions, assets and individual circumstances.

### Is Income Tax the same as National Insurance?

No. Income Tax and National Insurance are separate deductions with different rules and purposes.

### How much income is tax-free in the UK?

For someone eligible for the standard Personal Allowance, the first **£12,570** of income is generally covered by the Personal Allowance for 2025/26. The allowance can be reduced for people with higher incomes.

### Do you pay tax on your salary?

You may pay Income Tax and National Insurance on employment earnings depending on your income and circumstances.

### Why is tax deducted from my payslip?

If you are an employee, your employer normally deducts applicable Income Tax and National Insurance before paying your net salary. Income Tax is commonly collected through PAYE.

### What happens if I earn more money?

Higher income can move some of your taxable income into a higher tax band. This does not normally mean that your entire salary is taxed at the higher rate; different portions of taxable income can be taxed at different rates.

### Is tax the same across the UK?

Not completely. Scotland has different Income Tax rates and bands for most non-savings and non-dividend income. Other UK-wide taxes can also have their own rules and thresholds.

## Bottom Line

Tax is a system of compulsory payments used by governments to raise revenue. In the UK, there are several different taxes, including Income Tax, National Insurance, VAT, Capital Gains Tax, Corporation Tax and Inheritance Tax.

For employees, the most important starting point is understanding your **gross income, Personal Allowance, Income Tax bands and National Insurance deductions**.

Tax rules and rates can change between tax years, so always check the latest information from HMRC before making financial decisions.
