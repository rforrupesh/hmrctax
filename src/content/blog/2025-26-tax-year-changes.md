- Personal Allowance: £12,570 (tax-free)
- Basic Rate (20%): £12,571 to £50,270
- Higher Rate (40%): £50,271 to £125,140
- Additional Rate (45%): above £125,140

# 2025/26 UK Tax Year: What Changed?

The **2025/26 UK tax year** runs from **6 April 2025 to 5 April 2026**. While many of the main Income Tax rates and allowances remained unchanged, there were several important changes affecting employees, employers, dividends, National Insurance, savings and other areas of personal taxation.

For anyone calculating their UK tax bill, comparing salaries or planning their finances, understanding the **2025/26 tax rates and allowances** is important.

This guide explains what changed in the 2025/26 UK tax year and how the rules compare with 2024/25.

## When Does the 2025/26 UK Tax Year Start and End?

The 2025/26 tax year started on **6 April 2025** and ended on **5 April 2026**.

The UK tax year is different from the calendar year. For example, income earned between January and March 2026 belongs to the 2025/26 tax year, while income from 6 April 2026 belongs to the 2026/27 tax year.

If you are checking your tax using an online **[UK Tax Calculator](/uk-tax-calculator/)**, make sure you select the correct tax year.

## 2025/26 Income Tax Rates

For England, Wales and Northern Ireland, the main Income Tax rates for 2025/26 remained at 20%, 40% and 45%.

| Tax band | Taxable income | Rate |
|---|---:|---:|
| Personal Allowance | Up to £12,570 | 0% |
| Basic rate | £12,571–£50,270 | 20% |
| Higher rate | £50,271–£125,140 | 40% |
| Additional rate | Over £125,140 | 45% |

These figures assume the standard **£12,570 Personal Allowance**. HMRC confirms that the Personal Allowance remained £12,570 for 2025/26.

The taxable-income bands are technically £1–£37,700 for the basic-rate band, £37,701–£125,140 for the higher-rate band and over £125,140 for the additional-rate band. With the standard Personal Allowance, this produces the commonly used gross-income thresholds of £50,270 and £125,140.

## Did the Personal Allowance Change in 2025/26?

No. The standard Personal Allowance stayed at **£12,570**.

This means most taxpayers can receive up to £12,570 of adjusted net income before paying Income Tax.

The allowance starts to reduce when adjusted net income exceeds **£100,000**. It is reduced by £1 for every £2 of income above £100,000 and can eventually fall to zero.

For example, someone earning £110,000 could have their Personal Allowance reduced by £5,000, leaving a £7,570 allowance.

## National Insurance Changes in 2025/26

Employee Class 1 National Insurance rates were **8%** between the Primary Threshold and Upper Earnings Limit, and **2%** on earnings above the Upper Earnings Limit.

For 2025/26, the main weekly thresholds were:

| National Insurance threshold | 2025/26 |
|---|---:|
| Lower Earnings Limit | £125 per week |
| Primary Threshold | £242 per week |
| Upper Earnings Limit | £967 per week |
| Employee rate | 8% |
| Employee rate above UEL | 2% |

The employer Class 1 National Insurance rate was **15%** above the Secondary Threshold.

This is separate from Income Tax. Your payslip can therefore include both **Income Tax and National Insurance deductions**.

## Dividend Tax in 2025/26

The **Dividend Allowance** remained at £500 in 2025/26.

For dividends above the allowance, the rates were:

| Taxpayer band | Dividend tax rate 2025/26 |
|---|---:|
| Basic rate | 8.75% |
| Higher rate | 33.75% |
| Additional rate | 39.35% |

The dividend rates were unchanged from 2024/25.

This can be particularly important for company directors and people receiving dividends from shares.

## Personal Savings Allowance

The Personal Savings Allowance also remained unchanged.

Basic-rate taxpayers can generally receive up to **£1,000 of savings interest tax-free**, while higher-rate taxpayers can generally receive up to **£500**. Additional-rate taxpayers do not receive a Personal Savings Allowance.

There is also a starting rate for savings of 0% on up to £5,000 of savings income in circumstances where the rules allow it.

## Capital Gains Tax in 2025/26

Capital Gains Tax rates for individuals remained at:

| Type of gain | Basic-rate taxpayer | Higher-rate taxpayer |
|---|---:|---:|
| Most assets | 18% | 24% |
| Residential property gains | 18% | 24% |

The applicable rate depends on the taxpayer's circumstances and the type of asset.

If you sell shares, investments, property or another asset for more than its allowable cost, you may need to consider Capital Gains Tax.

## Scotland Has Different Income Tax Rates

One important point when discussing **UK tax rates for 2025/26** is that Scotland has its own Income Tax bands for most non-savings and non-dividend income.

For 2025/26, Scottish rates included:

| Scottish band | Rate |
|---|---:|
| Starter | 19% |
| Basic | 20% |
| Intermediate | 21% |
| Higher | 42% |
| Advanced | 45% |
| Top | 48% |

The thresholds also differ from those used in England, Wales and Northern Ireland.

So, a person living in Scotland should use Scottish Income Tax bands when calculating employment income rather than simply applying the England and Wales rates.

## What Actually Changed in 2025/26?

The biggest takeaway is that **many headline personal tax rates did not change**.

The standard Personal Allowance remained £12,570, while the main Income Tax rates remained 20%, 40% and 45% in England, Wales and Northern Ireland. Dividend tax rates and the £500 Dividend Allowance also remained unchanged.

However, National Insurance thresholds and employer National Insurance arrangements are important when comparing total employment costs, while Scotland continued to operate its separate Income Tax structure.

The government also published detailed tax legislation and rates for 2025/26 covering areas such as pensions, Capital Gains Tax, Inheritance Tax and business taxation.

## Example: How Much Tax Would You Pay on a £40,000 Salary?

Suppose you earn **£40,000** in England during the 2025/26 tax year and receive the standard Personal Allowance.

Your first £12,570 is covered by the Personal Allowance.

That leaves:

**£40,000 − £12,570 = £27,430 taxable income**

At the 20% basic rate:

**£27,430 × 20% = £5,486 Income Tax**

This is an Income Tax example only. Your actual payslip deduction would also depend on National Insurance and factors such as pension contributions, student loan repayments, benefits and your tax code.

| Basic Income Tax rate | 20% | 20% |

The comparison shows why it is important not to assume that every new tax year brings a major change to the headline Income Tax rates. HMRC's published historical tables confirm these figures for both years.
