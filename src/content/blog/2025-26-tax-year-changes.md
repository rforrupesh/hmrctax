A summary of the Income Tax and National Insurance thresholds that apply for the 2025 to 2026 UK tax year, what stayed the same, and what it actually means for your take-home pay.

## The headline: thresholds are frozen again

The government has kept Income Tax and National Insurance thresholds frozen for 2025/26, continuing the freeze that began in the 2021/22 tax year. Wages have kept rising with inflation, but the point at which you start paying tax — and the point at which you move into a higher band — hasn't moved. This is often called "fiscal drag": more of your income gets pulled into tax each year even though the rates and bands look unchanged on paper.

In practical terms, if you got a pay rise this year, a larger share of that increase is taxed than it would have been if thresholds had risen with inflation.

## Income Tax bands (England, Wales & Northern Ireland)

- **Personal Allowance:** £12,570 — tax-free
- **Basic Rate (20%):** £12,571 to £50,270
- **Higher Rate (40%):** £50,271 to £125,140
- **Additional Rate (45%):** above £125,140

If your income goes above £100,000, your Personal Allowance starts shrinking — you lose £1 of allowance for every £2 you earn over that limit, until it disappears completely at £125,140. This creates an effective marginal tax rate of 60% on income between £100,000 and £125,140, which catches a lot of people out.

### Scotland has its own bands

Scottish Income Tax uses different bands and rates, set separately by the Scottish Government. If you live in Scotland, use the "I pay Scottish Income Tax" option on the calculator to get an accurate figure, since the Scottish bands don't line up with the rest of the UK.

## National Insurance

Class 1 employee National Insurance is charged at:

- **8%** on earnings between £12,570 and £50,270 a year
- **2%** on earnings above £50,270

| Band | Rate | On earnings |
|---|---|---|
| Below Personal Allowance | 0% | Up to £12,570 |
| Employee NI (main rate) | 8% | £12,570 – £50,270 |
| Employee NI (upper rate) | 2% | Above £50,270 |

National Insurance is calculated per pay period (weekly or monthly), not as a single annual figure, so your exact deduction can vary slightly depending on how your pay is structured across the year — for example, if you get a bonus in one month.

## Student loan repayments

If you're repaying a Plan 2 student loan, an extra 9% is deducted from earnings above the repayment threshold, on top of Income Tax and National Insurance. This is separate from — and in addition to — the bands above, which is why it can come as a surprise on a payslip.

## What this means for your take-home pay

Because the thresholds haven't moved, most people won't see a change in the *rate* of tax they pay this year compared to last — but anyone whose salary has increased will keep more of that increase eaten up by tax than they would in a year with rising thresholds. If you're close to £50,270 or £100,000, it's worth checking exactly where you land, since crossing either of those points changes your effective tax rate more than a simple percentage increase would suggest.
